Supplementary Note 7: Independent-batch reproducibility bounds what
any perturbation readout can predict
Motivation
Supplementary Figure S12 reports that the GRQ virtual-perturbation direction does not predict the real
differential-expression response (ρ ≈ 0), while the mean latent-shift direction does (ρ ≈ 0.22). Those
comparisons ask how good the predictor is. This note asks the complementary question, which bounds
every predictor at once: how reproducible is the response itself in independent experimental batches? A
readout cannot be validated against a target that does not repeat.

Data and design
We use the published Perturb-seq resource GSE281048 (Jiang, L. et al., Nat. Cell Biol. 27, 505–517,
2025; Mixscale pipeline), which performs more than 1,500 perturbations split across six cell lines and
five signalling contexts. Its meta.data carries an experimental-batch field, Batch info, taking the
values Rep1 and Rep2 (129,917 and 106,689 cells in the TGFB context; 236,606 cells in total).

What the two batches are, and what they are not. Two features of the design fix the interpretation,
and both are reported because they cut in opposite directions.

    • The batches are not disjoint cell-line panels: all sixteen sample ID labels appear in both batches
      (roughly 80% of each label’s cells in Rep1, 65–70% in Rep2), so both batches sequence the same
      material rather than independent biological preparations. The paper’s own description is that the
      perturbations are “split across” the cell lines. We therefore treat Rep1/Rep2 as technical batches
      (experimental rounds) rather than biological replicates, and read the reproducibility below as a
      technical reproducibility.
    • One cell line, BXPC3, is absent from Rep1. This would be a cell-line confound if the compari-
      son used BXPC3; it does not, because BXPC3 contributes no (Rep1, Rep2) pair. Removing
      BXPC3 leaves the group count and the median unchanged (n = 260, r = +0.064).

Measurement
For every (cell line × target) group with at least twenty cells in both batches, the perturbed cells of each
batch are compared with that batch’s own non-targeting (NT) controls. Counts are normalised per group
to log(1 + CP10k) and the shift is the difference of group means, so the batch effect is not confounded
with the perturbation effect. Reproducibility is the Pearson correlation between the two batches’ shift
vectors across all genes.

Controls
Two controls bracket the measurement, and a third supplies the null.

    • Positive control (NT vs NT). The two batches’ NT controls agree at r = +0.9962 on average
      across the five fully shared cell lines (range +0.9943 to +0.9972). The batch effect on gene-level
      pseudobulk is therefore negligible, and the low values below are not a measurement artefact.
    • Mismatched-target null. Correlating a target’s Rep1 shift against a different target’s Rep2 shift,
      matched on cell line (2,000 permutations), gives a median of +0.0085 (TGFB) and +0.0039
      (IFNG).
    • Within-batch cross-target floor. Correlating two different targets’ shifts inside one batch gives a
      median of +0.2466 (TGFB).

both contexts fewer than 1% of groups exceed r = 0.9.

     Two features of these numbers matter more than their absolute size.
     First, the same-target agreement is below the within-batch cross-target floor. Two unrelated
perturbations measured in the same batch agree at +0.247, while the same perturbation measured in two
batches agrees at +0.064. Same-batch pairs evidently share a large common component — the batch’s
own state, plus technical structure — that does not survive across batches. The batch-stable part of a
single-gene signature is therefore smaller than the part that two arbitrary perturbations happen to share.
     Second, pathway membership does not raise reproducibility. Because each context’s target list is
a single signalling family, the natural objection is that the reproducible unit is the pathway rather than the
gene. We tested this directly, grouping each context’s targets into hand-built functional sets (listed per
context in the analysis code; 39/52 TGFB targets and 54/59 IFNG targets covered) and comparing cross-
batch correlations within a group against across groups. Within-group and across-group agreement are
indistinguishable: TGFB +0.0085 vs +0.0092 (difference −0.0007, Mann–Whitney p = 0.93); IFNG
+0.0063 vs +0.0058 (difference +0.0005). The same test inside a single batch also shows no advantage
(TGFB Rep1: +0.2669 vs +0.2605; Rep2: +0.2560 vs +0.2459). A small-sample coverage artefact
is excluded because the IFNG grouping was rebuilt after the TGFB grouping was found to cover only 3
of its 59 targets.

Relation to the internal split-half estimate
An internal estimate obtained by splitting one cell population in half reports a much higher number:
the same groups show a median split-half correlation of about 0.9 in this resource. The discrepancy is
not a contradiction of the measurement above; it identifies what split-half measures. Two halves of one
population share the library, the batch, and the technical noise of that batch, so a strong effect drives both
halves to the same noisy value and the correlation saturates. This is also why split-half reproducibility
saturates at 1 in datasets where the underlying cross-batch agreement is near zero. We therefore report
split-half values elsewhere in this work as an upper bound and a same-batch consistency check, not as
evidence that an effect reproduces.

Consequence for perturbation readouts
The reproducible component of a single-gene perturbation signature is a small fraction of its variance,
and it is not organised by pathway. Any readout — GRQ, latent-shift, decoder-direct, or a trained re-
sponse predictor — is bounded by this: the ceiling on agreement with a real response is set by how much
of that response is stable across batches, and no method can exceed it. This is the mechanistic counterpart
of the ρ ≈ 0 result for the GRQ direction in Supplementary Figure S12, and it is why that null should
not be read as a property of the GRQ construction alone. It also means that target-gene specificity and

Limits
(i) The batch field is interpreted as technical from design evidence (all sample ID labels present in
both batches; the source paper’s “split across” wording) rather than from an explicit author definition of
the field, which could not be retrieved. If the two batches are in fact biological replicates, the measured
value is a biological reproducibility and is not lower. (ii) Two contexts were analysed (TGFB, IFNG);
the remaining three GSE281048 contexts were not available locally. (iii) The pathway groupings are
hand-built functional sets by gene name, not database annotations; they are listed in full in the analysis
code so the grouping can be audited, and both contexts carry their own grouping. (iv) Correlations are
gene-level Pearson on log-normalised pseudobulk without expression filtering, so low-expression genes
contribute noise. (v) Reproducibility is estimated from two batches only, so no across-batch variance
component can be separated from within-batch variance.
